When researchers talk about adolescent marijuana use, the conversation often moves quickly toward individual risk factors. Age, peer influence, family circumstances, mental health, and attitudes toward marijuana all matter. But that list can leave out an important question: what happens when young people experience an institution as unfair, hostile, or indifferent to them?

My thesis, [*Institutional Strain and Substance Coping: Analyzing Perceived Racial Discrimination as a Predictor of Adolescent Marijuana Use*](/publications/institutional-strain), examines that question using the framework of General Strain Theory. The study asks whether perceived racial or ethnic discrimination is associated with psychological distress and marijuana use among U.S. high school students. The full thesis is available through the [University of Mississippi repository](https://egrove.olemiss.edu/etd/8839), and the [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/413720361_Institutional_Strain_and_Substance_Coping_Analyzing_Perceived_Racial_Discrimination_as_A_Predictor_of_Adolescent_Marijuana_Use) includes the public full text.

The argument is not that discrimination mechanically causes marijuana use. The design is cross-sectional, so it cannot establish temporal order or causality. The more careful argument is that institutional discrimination can operate as a serious strain, psychological distress is closely associated with marijuana use, and these relationships deserve to be studied together rather than in isolation.

## The Question Behind the Study

General Strain Theory, developed most fully by Robert Agnew, proposes that certain negative experiences create pressure, negative emotions, and incentives for coping. Strain is especially consequential when people experience it as unjust, severe, repeated, or difficult to escape (Agnew, 2001, 2006). Substance use is one possible form of coping, although it is not the only one and should not be assumed to be the response of every person who experiences strain.

The study extends that framework to perceived racial discrimination in school settings. Schools are not only places where students receive instruction. They are also institutions where young people encounter rules, authority, evaluation, discipline, belonging, and exclusion. When students believe that they are treated differently because of their race or ethnicity, that experience may affect both their emotional well-being and their relationship with the institution.

This perspective also connects criminological theory with research on racism and health. Williams and Mohammed (2013) describe racism as operating through multiple levels, including institutional policies and practices that shape access to education, employment, neighborhoods, and other resources. Treating discrimination as institutional strain does not reduce racism to an individual feeling. It asks how repeated experiences are produced and maintained through ordinary institutional arrangements.

## Why Call It Institutional Strain?

The word *institutional* is important. A single rude interaction can be painful, but institutional strain refers to a wider pattern. It can emerge through discipline, surveillance, unequal treatment, exclusion, or the expectation that a student will not receive the same benefit of the doubt as someone else.

General Strain Theory emphasizes that strain is more likely to produce negative outcomes when it is perceived as unjust, high in magnitude, repeated, and difficult to control (Agnew, 2001, 2006). Racial discrimination can contain all of these features. It can challenge a young person's sense of fairness, occur across multiple encounters, affect a core part of identity, and arise in settings that the young person cannot easily leave.

This is why the study does not treat perceived discrimination as a minor background variable. It is examined as a possible institutional stressor that may be associated with psychological distress and marijuana use. The framework is also compatible with a broader public-health concern: racism and discrimination can affect mental health through social conditions, not only through isolated interpersonal events (Williams & Mohammed, 2013).

## Data and Methods

The study uses the complete-case sample from the 2023 Youth Risk Behavior Survey. The analytic sample includes 17,268 high school students in the United States. The CDC describes the YRBS as a national surveillance system that tracks health behaviors and experiences among high school students, and its 2023 materials provide the survey documentation and national context for the data (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024).

The analysis uses logistic regression models to examine three connected questions:

- Is perceived racial or ethnic discrimination associated with psychological distress?
- Is perceived discrimination associated with marijuana use in the past month?
- Does the relationship change when psychological distress is included in the marijuana-use model, and do the patterns differ by sex?

The thesis also uses sex-stratified models. This matters because strain and coping are not necessarily experienced or expressed in the same way across groups. The analysis therefore asks whether the estimated associations differ for male and female students rather than treating the sample as completely uniform.

## What the Study Found

The results show a strong positive association between perceived discrimination and psychological distress. In the model predicting distress, perceived discrimination had an odds ratio of 2.34, with a 95 percent confidence interval from 2.18 to 2.51.

The marijuana-use models show a related pattern. Before psychological distress is added, perceived discrimination is associated with greater odds of past-month marijuana use, with an odds ratio of 1.78. After psychological distress is included, the estimated association for discrimination decreases to 1.44 but remains statistically significant. Psychological distress itself has a larger estimated association with marijuana use in the full model, with an odds ratio of 3.00.

The reduction in the discrimination coefficient is consistent with an intervening emotional pathway, but the thesis does not claim that it proves formal statistical mediation. A formal mediation analysis was not conducted. That distinction is important because a coefficient changing after another variable is added does not, by itself, establish a complete causal mechanism.

The sex-stratified results also show differences. Perceived discrimination is associated with marijuana use for both male and female students, but the estimated association is larger for male students. Psychological distress shows the opposite pattern in the study's estimates, with a larger association for female students. These results should be read as group-specific associations, not as fixed claims about how all boys or all girls cope with strain.

## What the Findings Do Not Prove

The study does not prove that discrimination causes an adolescent to use marijuana. The data are cross-sectional, the variables include self-reported experiences, and the analysis cannot fully establish temporal order. It is possible that unmeasured factors influence both perceived discrimination and marijuana use. The findings therefore support an association-based interpretation.

The study also does not claim that every young person who experiences discrimination will use marijuana. People respond to strain through many pathways, including seeking social support, confronting the source of the strain, withdrawing, internalizing distress, or using formal and informal services. Marijuana use is one possible coping-related outcome within a much larger social and psychological process.

These limitations are not reasons to dismiss the findings. They are reasons to state the contribution accurately. The study provides a national, theory-informed analysis of relationships that are often discussed separately. It shows why institutional discrimination, emotional distress, and adolescent substance use should be considered together in future research.

## Why the Findings Matter for Policy

If schools and other youth-serving institutions want to reduce substance-related harm, prevention cannot focus only on individual behavior. It also has to consider whether students experience the institution as fair, safe, and responsive.

That does not mean every school policy should be described as discriminatory whenever students disagree with it. It means that institutions should examine patterns in discipline, surveillance, access to support, referral practices, and student belonging. Policies that appear neutral on paper can have unequal effects in practice. Young people are also more likely to trust services when they believe that the people providing those services will treat them with dignity.

The practical implication is a layered approach. Schools can combine evidence-based substance-use education with mental-health support, anti-discrimination procedures, transparent discipline, culturally responsive counseling, and meaningful ways for students to report unfair treatment. Researchers should evaluate whether these interventions change both institutional experiences and substance-related outcomes.

The central lesson is straightforward: adolescent marijuana use cannot be understood only as an individual decision detached from social institutions. The environment in which young people learn, are judged, disciplined, and supported can shape the strains they experience and the coping options they see as available.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is institutional strain?

Institutional strain is a way of describing harmful, unjust, repeated, or difficult-to-escape pressure produced through institutional settings and practices. In this study, perceived racial discrimination is examined as one possible form of institutional strain.

### Does the study prove that discrimination causes marijuana use?

No. The study is cross-sectional and identifies statistical associations. It does not establish causality or prove that discrimination directly causes marijuana use.

### What data does the study use?

The study uses the complete-case sample from the 2023 Youth Risk Behavior Survey, with 17,268 U.S. high school students.

### What theory guides the research?

The study uses Robert Agnew's General Strain Theory, which explains how serious, unjust, repeated, or difficult-to-control strains may produce negative emotions and pressure for coping.
