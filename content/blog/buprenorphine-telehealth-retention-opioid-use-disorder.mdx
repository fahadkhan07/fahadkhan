The federal government has spent years debating whether buprenorphine can be prescribed through telehealth without an initial in-person visit. The debate is usually framed as a conflict between access and diversion control. That framing is incomplete. The more important policy question is whether people can start treatment and remain connected to it long enough for treatment to do its work.

In January 2025, the Drug Enforcement Administration and the Department of Health and Human Services finalized a rule allowing qualified practitioners to prescribe FDA-approved medications for opioid use disorder through telemedicine, including audio-only encounters under specified conditions. The rule requires a review of the relevant prescription-drug-monitoring-program data and sets conditions around the initial supply. The policy matters because a person does not need to travel to a clinic simply to cross the threshold into care.

But a legal pathway is not a treatment outcome. Recent research on a large telehealth-only buprenorphine program offers a useful, appropriately limited conclusion: virtual care can support substantial retention, yet retention varies sharply according to how treatment is initiated and whether people already have medication experience.

## The Policy Question After the Waiver

Buprenorphine is an evidence-based medication for opioid use disorder (OUD). It reduces withdrawal and craving and, when treatment is sustained, reduces the risk associated with illicit opioid use. Yet many people who could benefit from medication do not receive it. SAMHSA's 2024 National Survey on Drug Use and Health estimated that 4.8 million people aged 12 or older had past-year OUD, while 17.0 percent—about 818,000 people—received medication for OUD during the year.

The gap is not explained by a single obstacle. Treatment may be unavailable locally, unaffordable, stigmatizing, difficult to reconcile with work or caregiving, or structured around rules that make early treatment hard to sustain. Rural distance and transportation are obvious barriers, but urban availability does not guarantee practical access either. A clinic can exist and still be unreachable to a person without paid leave, stable housing, a private phone, or confidence that seeking help will not expose them to judgment or surveillance.

Telehealth changes the geography of the first appointment. It does not automatically solve the economics, stigma, pharmacy access, digital divide, or instability that follow it. That is why retention is a more revealing policy outcome than the number of telehealth prescriptions alone.

## What the New Retention Evidence Shows

Monico and colleagues' 2025 retrospective cohort study examined 22,064 adults who initiated OUD treatment in a multistate, telehealth-only program between May 2019 and April 2025. The cohort included substantial numbers of rural residents and Medicaid enrollees: 33.8 percent lived in rural areas and 78.5 percent were enrolled in Medicaid.

Retention was 86.8 percent at month one and 71.0 percent at month six, then declined gradually through 24 months. At six months, retention was higher among participants who had already been receiving buprenorphine when they entered the program than among those transitioning to treatment. Among people with prior buprenorphine exposure, 70.4 percent remained engaged at month six, compared with 52.0 percent among a transition group; the reported risk difference was 18.5 percentage points.

The study also found differences by treatment pathway. A high-dose initiation pathway showed the highest early retention, while people already stabilized on buprenorphine had higher retention than all transition pathways. These results are clinically and politically interesting, but they are not a randomized comparison of telehealth with in-person treatment. The program's patients may differ from people who never seek care, and the program's clinical model may not represent every virtual provider.

The proper conclusion is therefore modest. Telehealth-delivered buprenorphine can retain many patients, including people in rural areas and people receiving Medicaid. The study does not prove that telehealth is universally superior, nor does it show that a prescription by itself solves the treatment gap.

## Access Is Not the Same as Continuity

Public debate often treats access as a binary: either a person can obtain a prescription or cannot. OUD care is more accurately understood as a sequence of possible drop-off points. A person must find a clinician, complete an intake, begin medication, obtain it from a pharmacy, manage side effects or withdrawal, attend follow-up, and navigate changes in housing, insurance, transportation, phone access, or drug supply. A policy that removes one barrier may still leave the others intact.

This is why the study's pathway differences matter. People entering virtual treatment while already receiving buprenorphine may have a more stable relationship with medication and fewer immediate induction challenges. New initiates may need more intensive support, faster follow-up, flexible dosing conversations, and help when the first plan does not work. If policymakers count only the number of clinicians allowed to prescribe, they miss the institutional design of continuity.

The same caution applies to safety claims. The 2025 federal rule creates safeguards, but no rule can guarantee that every provider offers high-quality care or that every patient can obtain medication after a telehealth visit. Diversion concerns should be addressed through clinically appropriate monitoring and accountability, not by recreating unnecessary barriers that make treatment inaccessible. Conversely, a low-threshold model should still provide informed consent, medication education, clinical follow-up, and routes to urgent care.

## Why Low-Threshold Care Matters

Harm reduction is sometimes mischaracterized as an alternative to treatment. In practice, low-threshold buprenorphine can be a way of making treatment more reachable for people who are not well served by abstinence-first or highly conditional systems. The objective is not to romanticize medication or ignore clinical judgment. It is to reduce the number of institutional hurdles between a person and an effective intervention.

That institutional question is also a criminal-justice question. People who use drugs often interact with hospitals, police, courts, probation agencies, child-welfare systems, and treatment providers under conditions of unequal power. If seeking medication is associated with surveillance, sanctions, or loss of autonomy, a formally available service may remain practically unsafe. A trustworthy treatment system needs clear confidentiality rules, nonpunitive referral pathways, and separation between care and routine enforcement wherever law permits.

Telehealth can help by reducing travel and the visibility of entering a clinic. It can also reproduce exclusion when a person lacks broadband, a private room, a compatible device, digital literacy, or a reliable pharmacy. Audio-only options may be especially important for continuity, but they should not be treated as a cheap substitute for broader care infrastructure. The relevant question is not whether virtual treatment is inherently good or bad. It is whether a particular design makes sustained, dignified care more possible.

## What Better Policy Would Measure

An evidence-informed policy would evaluate more than prescription volume. It would track:

- initiation and six- and twelve-month retention, with results separated by treatment pathway;
- pharmacy access, medication interruptions, and time from first contact to first dose;
- rurality, insurance, race and ethnicity, disability, housing stability, and digital access;
- patient-reported trust, stigma, privacy, and ability to contact a clinician quickly;
- emergency-department use, overdose events, and linkage to naloxone and other harm-reduction services; and
- whether monitoring practices improve safety without pushing people out of care.

This agenda also requires better comparison. Retrospective program data can show who stays connected, but quasi-experimental and prospective studies can better assess how virtual-only, hybrid, and in-person models perform under different local conditions. Researchers should report who is missing from the data: people without stable internet, people who leave treatment quickly, and people who never reach a provider.

The policy lesson is straightforward. Keep telehealth access broad enough to reach people who cannot easily attend a clinic, but invest in the relationships and practical supports that make retention possible. A six-month treatment connection is more meaningful than a one-time prescription, and a system that retains people is safer than one that merely advertises availability.

## Conclusion

The 2025 federal buprenorphine telemedicine rule is an important access reform, but it should not be mistaken for a complete OUD treatment strategy. Recent multistate evidence suggests that telehealth-only care can retain many patients, while also showing that retention differs across entry pathways and is lower for some people beginning treatment.

The strongest case for telehealth is therefore neither technological enthusiasm nor regulatory fear. It is practical: virtual care can remove distance and visibility barriers, especially when it is low threshold, clinically accountable, and connected to pharmacies, naloxone, emergency care, and human follow-up. Drug policy should measure whether people remain alive and connected to effective care—not simply whether a rule allows a prescription to be written.
