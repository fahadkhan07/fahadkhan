Cannabis legalization is often presented as a way to move consumers from an illicit market into a regulated one. That goal is plausible, but it contains a policy assumption that deserves closer examination: legal status is not the same thing as practical access.

A state can authorize adult use while offering few stores, high prices, limited product availability, inconvenient locations, or rules that exclude people who previously relied on informal suppliers. Under those conditions, legalization may coexist with a substantial illicit market. The important question is therefore not simply whether cannabis is legal. It is whether the regulated market is usable enough to compete with the market it is supposed to replace.

## The Policy Assumption Behind Legalization

The [research on illegal drug markets and cannabis legalization](/blog/institutional-myths-drug-policy-enforcement) shows why the relationship is not one-directional. Legalization can reduce some forms of illicit supply, but it can also create new forms of diversion, gray-market activity, and enforcement difficulty. A regulated market is not a switch that turns an illicit market off. It is an institution competing with existing relationships, prices, convenience, product variety, and trust.

This distinction matters for harm reduction. Licensed sellers can provide tested products, standardized labeling, age checks, and health information. But those protections reach consumers only when consumers can and will use licensed outlets. If legal access is geographically sparse or financially unattractive, the people most exposed to untested products may remain outside the regulated system.

The alternative is not to romanticize illicit markets. Informal supply can expose consumers to contamination, unpredictable potency, violence, legal sanctions, and no meaningful recourse when something goes wrong. The point is institutional: prohibition and legalization both shape the conditions under which people obtain drugs, and regulation succeeds only when its protections are available in practice.

## What the Ohio Evidence Adds

Xu and colleagues provide unusually timely evidence from a policy transition in Ohio. Their 2026 study uses three waves of panel data collected in June 2024, January 2025, and August 2025. The analytic sample included 578 adults observed across the waves. Ohio began licensed recreational retail sales in August 2024, creating a useful before-and-after policy shock for examining changes in where people obtained cannabis.

The study uses a difference-in-differences design and compares the share of cannabis respondents obtained from legal sources after retail sales began. Its central contribution is behavioral rather than rhetorical: it examines market switching among consumers instead of treating legalization itself as evidence that switching occurred.

That design still has limits. The sample is not a census of all cannabis consumers, self-reported sourcing can be imperfect, and a recent policy transition cannot answer every long-term question about prices, product safety, or criminal-justice outcomes. The study also does not mean that Ohio represents every state. Its value is narrower and more useful: it shows how researchers can test the displacement claim directly, and it highlights that the outcome depends on implementation.

## Legalization Is Not the Same as Access

The Ohio case is especially instructive because retail authorization unfolds over time. A state may pass a law on one date, issue licenses later, open stores later still, and gradually expand the range and geographic distribution of products. Consumers experience those stages differently. “Legal” may initially mean that possession is lawful, while the nearest licensed store remains far away or cannot meet demand.

This is why studies of dispensary availability matter. Research in Washington has linked geographic access to legal retailers with cannabis-use patterns, while California research has documented the simultaneous presence of licensed and unlicensed retailers. Those findings should not be collapsed into a simple claim that more stores always produce more use or that fewer stores always protect public health. They show that retail geography is a policy variable with consequences for who can reach the regulated system.

Price is another access variable. Taxes and compliance costs can finance public programs and improve product oversight, but a large legal-market price premium can preserve demand for illicit suppliers. The policy challenge is not to eliminate every price difference at any cost. It is to set taxes and licensing rules with enough attention to consumer behavior that regulation remains a credible alternative to unregulated supply.

## Why Consumers Stay in Mixed Markets

Consumers do not necessarily choose one market permanently. They may buy some products from licensed stores and others from informal suppliers. A person may value laboratory testing for concentrates, lower prices for flower, a nearby seller’s convenience, or the privacy of a familiar relationship. Someone who cannot legally purchase because of age, immigration concerns, prior convictions, or local store scarcity may also remain in the illicit market even after legalization.

The result is a mixed market rather than a clean transition. Mixed-market behavior complicates evaluation: a decline in arrests does not prove that illicit sourcing has disappeared, and an increase in legal sales does not reveal how many consumers have switched, supplemented, or simply increased total consumption.

It also cautions against enforcement-only responses. If policymakers define the remaining illicit market solely as a compliance failure, they may respond with raids without addressing the legal market's price, distance, licensing delays, or exclusionary design. Enforcement can be necessary to protect communities from dangerous commercial activity, but it cannot substitute for a regulated market that people can realistically use.

## What Regulators Should Measure

An evidence-based cannabis policy should track more than legal sales and seizure totals. Regulators should publish, where privacy protections permit:

- the share of cannabis consumers obtaining products from legal, illicit, and mixed sources;
- retail availability by neighborhood, rurality, transportation access, and income;
- legal and illicit price differences for comparable products;
- product-test results, recalls, and adverse-event reports;
- licensing delays, business survival, and barriers facing social-equity applicants; and
- whether enforcement changes reduce harmful supply or merely displace it geographically.

These measures should be paired with health and criminal-justice outcomes. A market can displace some illicit sales while increasing heavy use, unequal exposure to commercial marketing, or burdens on communities targeted by enforcement. Conversely, a market can fall short of total displacement while still reducing arrests and improving product information for many consumers. Good evaluation has to hold those possibilities together.

## Conclusion

The strongest lesson from the emerging evidence is simple: cannabis legalization and cannabis-market transition are different policy questions. Legalization establishes a legal category. Retail access, affordability, product quality, geography, trust, and inclusion determine whether people can actually use that category.

The Ohio panel study offers a timely way to examine the transition rather than assume it. Its findings should be read alongside research on retail geography, prices, unlicensed sellers, and mixed sourcing. No single study can settle the future of illicit cannabis markets, but the direction of better policy is clear: measure consumer pathways, design regulation around real access, and treat displacement as an empirical outcome—not a promise embedded in the word “legalization.”
